Session 3
Competency-Based HR & Assessment Centers

This session introduces competency as the fundamental currency of modern HR — shifting the lens from what a person has done to what a person is capable of doing, and explains how Assessment Centers provide a multi-method environment to observe that capability directly.

Core Definitions
CompetencyA cluster of related, measurable abilities, skills, knowledge, expertise, commitments, and personality traits that enable a person to perform successfully in a job or situation.
Assessment CenterA standardized evaluation using multiple tools and trained assessors to measure complex behavioral competencies. Goal: assess what candidates will actually do, not just how well they interview.
Psychometric AssessmentTests developed by trained psychologists measuring cognitive ability (abstract, verbal, numerical reasoning), personality, and interests. Can be standalone or part of an AC.
Why Competency-Based HR Exists
The Problem with Traditional Hiring

Traditional selection relied on resumes, education credentials, and short interviews — instruments that reveal only ~10% of a person. The 90% that drives real performance (reasoning style, motivations, behavioral patterns under pressure) remained invisible.

Assessment Centers were originally developed for military intelligence officer selection, because those roles demanded complex behaviors no single test could capture. The solution: observe candidates multiple times, in multiple realistic scenarios, rated by multiple trained assessors.

Visual — The Total Person / 10–90 Iceberg
What Standard Hiring Sees vs What Assessment Centers Reach
The 3 Performance Drivers
01
Ability
Cognitive and technical capacity — skills, knowledge, reasoning. An employee may want to perform but lack the tools. Ability is what training and education develop.
02
Willingness
Motivation, attitude, engagement. An employee may be fully capable but disengaged. Lives mostly in the subconscious — invisible on a CV. This is what behavioral interviews surface.
03
External Factors
Resources, tools, leadership quality, team dynamics. Even a capable, willing employee underperforms when the environment fails them. Often misdiagnosed as a people problem.
Manager insight: Before training, incentivizing, or replacing someone — identify which driver is failing. Training fixes Ability. Rewards fix Willingness. Process redesign fixes External Factors. Misdiagnosing wastes resources.

CBHR Framework — 4 Pillars
The Core Paradigm Shift: Job-Centered → Output-Centered

Traditional HR focuses on job descriptions and tasks. CBHR focuses on the outputs of exemplary performers and maps back to the competencies that produce those outputs. This transforms HR from an administrative cost center into a strategic value driver.

Traditional (Job-Based)
Focus: Job descriptions & task lists
Approach: Reactive — fill vacancies as they arise
Evaluation: Effort and time spent
Development: Generic training catalog
Pay: Based on seniority and job title
VS
Modern (Competency-Based)
Focus: Competency models & measurable results
Approach: Proactive — anticipate future skill needs
Evaluation: Measurable productivity & outcomes
Development: Targeted IDPs to close specific gaps
Pay: Based on competency mastery and impact
Strategic Recruitment & Selection — Hire for outputs of exemplary performers, not task history. Use behavioral assessments.
Dynamic Performance Management — Continuous feedback, competency assessment, productivity metrics. Not annual rituals.
Rewards for Exemplary Performance — Pay tied to competency mastery and measurable productivity, not just tenure or title.
Individual Development Plans (IDP) — Personalized roadmap to close competency gaps. Lifelong learning mindset.
CBI Interview — STAR Technique
STAR Method: Situation → Task → Action → Result. Always probe with open questions (never leading or closed). Use the OPEN PROBE → Information Exchange → STAR → CHECK → Reflect Back cycle.
  • Ask open-ended questions; avoid leading/multiple/personal questions
  • Summarize what the applicant says; pause after every question
  • Look for trends and supportive evidence across answers
  • Do not argue or give advice to the interviewee

Job Analysis & Job Design

Job Analysis is the foundation of almost every HR function. You cannot recruit, train, evaluate, or fairly compensate without first knowing precisely what a job requires. This session explains both the process and the trends reshaping it.

Core Definitions
Job AnalysisProcedure to determine the duties, skill requirements, and type of person needed for a job — the master HR tool.
Job DescriptionList of a job's duties, responsibilities, reporting relationships, working conditions, and supervisory responsibilities.

Job Design — Theory & 3 Strategies
The Logic of Job Design

Job design determines how work is structured to maximize both organizational productivity and employee motivation. The three strategies differ in direction: horizontal (more tasks at the same level), vertical (more responsibility and autonomy), or lateral (movement across roles).

↔
Job Enlargement
Horizontal expansion. Adding more tasks at the same complexity level. Reduces monotony but doesn't increase responsibility. More variety, same challenge.
↑
Job Enrichment
Vertical expansion. Adds decision-making authority, ownership, and meaningful challenge. Based on Herzberg's motivators: achievement, recognition, growth.
⟳
Job Rotation
Lateral movement. Moves employees across departments to build breadth, identify strengths, and prepare high-potentials. Reduces single-point-of-failure risk.
2025 Trends in Job Analysis

76% of orgs adopting skills-based approach; dynamic skill profiles replace static JDs
2. AI Workflow Redesign
80% deployed AI; only 20% rebuilt workflows. Human-centric redesign is the gap.
3. Reinvesting AI Capacity
AI frees time → reinvest into innovation, empathy-led leadership, complex problem solving
4. Strategic Segmentation
Prioritize "critical workforce segments" — high-demand/low-supply skills
5. Internal Talent Marketplaces
Flow-based work matching, agile 3-year pathfinder strategies, relational analytics

Session 5
Recruitment, Selection & Placement

Recruitment generates a pool. Selection filters it. Together they form a sequential series of hurdles — each stage eliminates candidates — culminating in the hiring decision. Understanding the yield at each stage is what makes recruitment planning quantitative rather than guesswork.

The 5-Step Recruitment & Selection Process
Decide what positions to fill — personnel planning & forecasting
Build candidate pool — recruit internal or external candidates
Candidates complete application forms & initial screening interviews
Use selection tools to identify viable candidates
Decide who to offer — supervisors interview final candidates
Visual — The Recruiting Yield Pyramid
Filtering from Leads to Hires — Work Backwards from Target
Work backwards: Need 10 hires? 50% offer acceptance = 20 offers. 67% of interviewees get offers = ~30 interviews needed. 75% pass screening = ~40 invited. 16% yield from leads = ~250 leads needed. Plan your sourcing accordingly.
Forecasting Techniques
Trend AnalysisStudy past employment levels to predict future needs based on historical staffing patterns.
Ratio AnalysisUse productivity ratios (e.g., sales per employee) to project required headcount.
Scatter PlotGraphically examine the relationship between a business activity metric and staffing level.
Succession PlanningProcess to fill the company's most important executive jobs — forward-looking talent pipeline.

Interview Types — Theory & Formats
Why Interview Structure Matters

Unstructured interviews have notoriously low predictive validity — interviewers are swayed by appearance, similarity bias, and first impressions. Research shows structured interviews double the predictive validity of unstructured ones. Situational questions ("What would you do if...?") test judgment. Behavioral questions ("Tell me about a time when...") test actual demonstrated behavior — the best predictor of future performance.

Dimension Options

Content Situational / Behavioral / Job-Related / Stress
Administration Sequential, Panel, Mass, Computerized, Phone, Video/Web

Performance Management & Appraisal

Performance Appraisal is an event. Performance Management is an ongoing system. The central theoretical insight: PM represents the evolution beyond traditional appraisal — continuous, forward-looking, and strategically aligned rather than periodic and backward-looking.

Key Distinction
Performance Appraisal
What: Setting standards, assessing performance, providing feedback
When: Periodic event (annual/semi-annual)
Focus: Backward-looking — "How did you do?"
Purpose: Rate and record
VS
Performance Management
What: Integrated approach ensuring performance supports strategic aims
When: Continuous, ongoing process
Focus: Forward-looking — "How do we improve?"
Purpose: Develop and align

Why BARS Is the Gold Standard

BARS replaces vague descriptors ("excellent," "poor") with concrete behavioral examples at each rating level. When a manager gives a "3," everyone — manager, employee, auditor — knows exactly what behavior that means. This eliminates ambiguity that makes most rating scales legally and statistically weak. Developing BARS: write critical incidents → develop dimensions → reallocate incidents → scale them → finalize instrument.

Lewin's 3-Stage Change Model — Deep Theory
Why Change Fails Without This Framework

People's behaviors are held in place by a balance of driving forces and restraining forces. Lewin's model addresses all three stages: creating motivation to change (Unfreeze), making the actual change (Move), and locking in new behaviors so people don't revert (Refreeze).

Most failed change initiatives skip Unfreeze (mandate change without buy-in) or skip Refreeze (implement the change but don't reinforce habits, so people drift back within months).

Unfreeze — Disrupt the status quo. Create urgency. Mobilize commitment. Help people see why the old way no longer works. Without this, people resist even clearly beneficial changes.
Move (Change) — Build guiding coalition. Develop & communicate shared vision. Provide resources and training. Consolidate small wins to build momentum.
Refreeze — Institutionalize the new way. Update systems and policies to reinforce new behaviors. Monitor progress. Recognize and reward people who model the change.
